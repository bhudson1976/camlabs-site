How to Tell If a PDF's Redactions Are Fake (Before Someone Else Does)

By Bryon Hudson · Founder, CamLabs AI
October 9, 2026 · 4 min read

A redacted PDF can look perfect on screen and still hand over every word you meant to hide. If the black boxes were drawn on top of the text instead of replacing it, anyone can select the "blacked out" area, copy it and paste it somewhere else.

It happens to courts, agencies and big firms

This isn't a rookie mistake that only happens to small offices. A few public examples:

  • Federal court filings, 2019. Lawyers for Paul Manafort filed a response with black boxes over sensitive passages. Reporters copied and pasted the text and read what was underneath. The filing was pulled and replaced (ABA Judges' Journal).
  • A federal judge's opinion, 2011. Redacted parts of an opinion in Apple v. Samsung could be copied out, revealing business details. The court sealed it and posted a fixed version within hours (same source).
  • Court records at scale. A Princeton researcher scanned 1.8 million documents on PACER, the federal court records system, and found 194 with redactions that didn't work. He estimated thousands more across the full system (ABA Journal).
  • Government releases, 2025. After the Justice Department released the Epstein files, people found that some redactions could be bypassed by copying the blacked-out text and pasting it into a document (Daily Dot).

Why a black box isn't a redaction

A PDF is built in layers. The words sit in one layer, and anything you draw, like a black rectangle or a thick highlight, sits in a layer on top. Drawing a box covers the words for your eyes. It doesn't remove them from the file. A true redaction deletes the text and anything else under the box, then saves a file where that content no longer exists.

The trap is that both look exactly the same on screen. You can't tell them apart by looking.

Three ways to check a PDF

  1. The copy test. Open the exported PDF, drag across a black bar and copy (Command-C on a Mac, Ctrl-C on Windows). Paste into a blank note. If any words show up, the redaction failed.
  2. The search test. Press Command-F or Ctrl-F and search for something you removed, like the last four digits of an account number. If the viewer finds it, it's still in the file.
  3. The free checker. Drop the PDF into our PDF redaction checker. It looks at every page, lists any text still hiding under dark boxes, flags redaction marks that were never applied, and shows the author and title saved inside the file. It runs in your browser and the file is never uploaded.

The first two tests only check the spot you test. The checker goes through every page, which matters on a 40-page filing.

Don't forget what's hiding in the file itself

Even a properly redacted PDF can carry details in its document info, like the original file name, the author's name or the title. Open the file's properties (File > Get Info or Document Properties) and check them before you send it. What to do if a file fails

Don't try to patch the bad file. Go back to the original, redact it with a tool that actually removes the text, and export a new copy. Then check the new copy again. No automated tool is perfect, including ours, so a final look at every page is still worth it.
