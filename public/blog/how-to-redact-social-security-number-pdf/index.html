How to Redact a Social Security Number in a PDF

By Bryon Hudson · CamLabs AI
October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

A Social Security number is the one detail an identity thief needs most. Tax returns, W-2s, HR files, loan applications and court exhibits all carry them, and those files get emailed, uploaded and filed every day.

The problem is that most people “redact” by drawing a black box over the number. On screen it looks gone. In the file, it is still there. This guide shows how to remove an SSN from a PDF for real, how to prove it worked, and what federal courts require.

Why a black box is not a redaction

A PDF stores text and drawings in separate layers. A black rectangle, black highlighting, or a black-filled comment sits on top of the text without deleting it. Anyone can select the area, copy, and paste the hidden number into a text editor.

This mistake keeps happening in high-stakes places. The ABA’s Judges’ Journal documented supposedly redacted filings in the Paul Manafort case and the Apple v. Samsung litigation that revealed their hidden text through simple copy and paste (ABA). In September 2025, law professor Eugene Volokh described the same failure in a filing from a large law firm, and noted that search engines can index the “hidden” text (Reason / Volokh Conspiracy).

A real redaction deletes the characters themselves, then covers the spot so readers can see something was removed.

Where SSNs hide in a PDF

Before you redact, know where to look. An SSN can appear in more places than the obvious form field:

  • Formatted three ways: 123-45-6789, 123 45 6789, or 123456789. Search for all three patterns, not just the dashed one.
  • Headers, footers and stamps: HR and tax forms often repeat the number on every page.
  • Scanned pages: a scan is a picture, so Ctrl+F won’t find the number unless the page has been through OCR (text recognition).
  • Form fields and comments: fillable fields and sticky notes can hold text that isn’t visible in the page view.
  • Attachments and metadata: embedded files, the document title, or bookmarks can carry a name or number.
  • Related identifiers: the same pages usually include a date of birth, address, or account number that also needs attention.

How to redact an SSN in a PDF, step by step

These steps work in any true redaction tool — one that deletes the underlying text, not one that only draws shapes. Adobe Acrobat Pro’s Redact tool is one example; there are others, including free ones.

  1. Work on a copy. Keep the original file untouched in case you need to redo the job.
  2. Make scanned pages searchable. If the PDF came from a scanner or phone camera, run OCR first so the tool can find the numbers.
  3. Search for every SSN pattern. Look for dashed, spaced and unbroken nine-digit numbers. Also search for the person’s name and “SSN” or “Social Security.”
  4. Mark each match for redaction. Use the tool’s redaction marker, not the highlighter, shape, or comment tools.
  5. Review every page by eye. Search misses handwriting, odd fonts and numbers split across lines. Check headers, footers, tables and signature blocks.
  6. Decide: full or partial. For court filings, federal rules allow the last four digits to stay visible (see below). Otherwise, remove the whole number.
  7. Apply the redactions. In most tools, marking is not the same as applying. Nothing is deleted until you apply or burn in the redactions.
  8. Remove hidden information. Clear metadata, comments, form data, bookmarks and attachments that could contain the number.
  9. Flatten and save as a new file. Flattening merges layers so nothing sits underneath the black box. Give the file a clear name, such as Exhibit-3-redacted.pdf.

How to check that the SSN is really gone

Never send a redacted file without testing it. A column in the ABA’s Judges’ Journal recommends a simple copy-paste test (ABA). It takes under two minutes:

  1. Open the redacted file, not the working copy.
  2. Press Ctrl+A (Cmd+A on Mac) to select all, then copy.
  3. Paste into a plain text editor such as Notepad or TextEdit.
  4. Search the pasted text for the full SSN, the first five digits, and the person’s name.
  5. Back in the PDF, use Find for the same strings, and try clicking or dragging across each black box.

If the number shows up anywhere, the redaction failed. Go back to the original and start again with a tool that applies redactions.

SSNs in court filings: the last-four rule

If you’re filing in federal court, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 5.2(a) says that, unless the court orders otherwise, a filing may include only the last four digits of a Social Security or taxpayer-identification number. The same rule limits birth dates to the year, minors to their initials, and financial-account numbers to the last four digits (Cornell LII, FRCP 5.2). Criminal Rule 49.1 and Bankruptcy Rule 9037 contain parallel provisions (Judicial Conference report, 2026).

A few points paralegals and filers should know:

  • The filer is responsible. Courts generally put the burden of redacting on the person filing, not the clerk.
  • There are exemptions. Rule 5.2(b) exempts some records, such as state-court records and administrative records (Cornell LII).
  • You can waive only your own protection. Under Rule 5.2(h), filing your own information unredacted and not under seal waives protection for it.
  • Check local rules. State courts and individual districts may have their own requirements.

A change is coming. In 2026, federal rules committees approved publishing proposed amendments that would require complete redaction of SSNs and ITINs in civil and criminal filings; bankruptcy filings would keep the last-four rule (Judicial Conference report, 2026). These are proposals, not yet law. If you’re unsure, redacting the full number is the safer choice wherever the last four digits aren’t needed.

This is general information, not legal advice. Confirm the rules for your court before you file.
